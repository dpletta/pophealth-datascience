11.1 Overview

NoteThe task: use several years of a survey in one analysis

The earlier chapters in this part analyze one year of the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS). There are two common reasons to use several years:

  • More respondents. One year may have too few respondents in a small group (young adults in one state, for example). Pooling years gives a more precise estimate of the group’s average over the period.
  • Change over time. Comparing years shows whether the population changed.

These are different questions, and they do not use the same weights. This chapter combines BRFSS 2022, 2023 and 2024 and estimates the prevalence of reported diagnosed diabetes both ways. Six things have to be right:

  1. each year asked the same question, with the same variable name and codes;
  2. the yearly files are stacked, not joined (Chapter 5);
  3. each year is in the file with its own strata and primary sampling units (PSUs);
  4. you know which states are in which years;
  5. the weights match the question: adjusted weights for an average over the years, and each year’s original weights for that year’s estimate;
  6. the design is declared on every respondent, and subgroups are selected from the design.
ImportantSeveral survey years are not longitudinal data

BRFSS draws a new sample every year. Nobody is interviewed again on purpose, and the files have no identifier that links a person across years. Stacked years are repeated cross-sections: they show how the population changed, not how individuals changed.

A panel (longitudinal) survey interviews the same people more than once. Its documentation usually supplies longitudinal weights for the people who responded at a given set of waves, which account for people who dropped out. Those weights, and the models that handle repeated answers from one person, are a different problem. The weight adjustment in this chapter is for repeated cross-sections only. For a panel survey, follow that survey’s own weighting documentation.

11.7 Step 5: Give each year its own strata and PSUs

Each year’s sample is drawn separately, so the years are independent samples. Variance estimation compares PSUs within a stratum (Chapter 7), and it must only compare respondents from the same year’s sample. The stratum codes do not ensure that, because the same codes are used every year:

Show / hide code 
strata_by_year <- split(stacked$STSTR, stacked$year)
# Number of stratum codes that are in both the 2022 and the 2024 file:
length(intersect(strata_by_year[["2022"]], strata_by_year[["2024"]]))
[1] 2046

If STSTR were used as it is, the survey package would treat stratum 11011 in 2022 and stratum 11011 in 2024 as one stratum. Numbering each year-stratum pair, and each year-stratum-PSU triple, keeps the years apart. This is the PSU identifier from Chapter 6 with the year added.

Show / hide code 
stacked <- stacked |>
  dplyr::mutate(strata_year = dplyr::cur_group_id(), .by = c(year, STSTR)) |>
  dplyr::mutate(psu_id = dplyr::cur_group_id(), .by = c(year, STSTR, PSU))

# Check: every new stratum belongs to one year, and every PSU identifier to one
# stratum (PSUs are nested in strata, and strata in years).
stopifnot(

    dplyr::n_distinct(stacked$strata_year),
  nrow(dplyr::distinct(stacked, psu_id, strata_year)) ==
    dplyr::n_distinct(stacked$psu_id)

NoteHow much does this matter?

In BRFSS each respondent is their own PSU and the strata are large, so merging strata across years changes the standard errors here only slightly. In a survey with a few large PSUs in each stratum, merging strata or PSUs from different years can change them a lot. Year-specific identifiers are correct in both cases and cost one line of code.

11.8 Step 6: Adjust the weights for an average over the years

Each year’s weights add up to the adult population that year’s file represents, in millions:

Show / hide code 
stacked |>
  dplyr::summarise(adults_millions = sum(LLCPWT) / 1e6, .by = year)
# A tibble: 3 × 2
   year adults_millions
  <int>           <dbl>
1  2022            265.
2  2023            254.
3  2024            264.

Stacked, the three years add up to about 783 million: the adult population counted three times. To describe an average year in the period, the weights have to be scaled down so that the years together count once. CDC’s guidance, in the section “Combining Multiple Years of BRFSS Data” of Complex Sampling Weights and Preparing Module Data for Analysis, gives two ways:

  • if the years have very similar sample sizes, divide each weight by the number of years;
  • otherwise, multiply each year’s weights by that year’s share of the combined sample.

CDC’s worked example is one state, New York, whose sample was 17,800 in 2022 and 43,913 in 2024. For a file with many states, this chapter applies the second rule within each state, as Section 5.2 does for questionnaire versions:

\[ \text{weight\_pooled} = \text{LLCPWT} \times \frac{n_\text{state, year}}{n_\text{state, all years}} \]

There are two reasons to work within states. Sample sizes change from year to year in some states and not in others. And a state that is in two of the three years gets shares that add up to 1 across its two years, so it still counts once. With one share for each year across the whole file, Kentucky, Pennsylvania and Tennessee would count for about two thirds of their populations.

Show / hide code 
stacked <- stacked |>
  dplyr::add_count(STATE, name = "n_state") |>
  dplyr::add_count(STATE, year, name = "n_state_year") |>
  dplyr::mutate(
    year_share = n_state_year / n_state,
    weight_pooled = LLCPWT * year_share
  )

shares <- stacked |>
  dplyr::distinct(STATE, state_name, year, n_state_year, n_state, year_share)
# Check: within every state, the year shares add up to 1.
share_totals <- dplyr::summarise(shares, total = sum(year_share), .by = STATE)
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(share_totals$total, rep(1, nrow(share_totals)))))

11.8.1 How much does the choice matter?

The share rule is a choice, so compare it with the alternatives. Table 11.1 gives the population each set of weights adds up to and the estimated prevalence of diabetes over the three years.

Show / hide code 
# Each respondent's year's share of the whole stacked file.
year_counts <- dplyr::count(stacked, year, name = "n_year")
whole_file_share <- year_counts$n_year[match(stacked$year, year_counts$year)] /
  nrow(stacked)

weight_options <- list(
  "Unadjusted" = stacked$LLCPWT,
  "One share for each year, whole file" = stacked$LLCPWT * whole_file_share,
  "Share within each state (used here)" = stacked$weight_pooled,
  "Equal shares within each state" = stacked$LLCPWT / stacked$years_in
)
weight_comparison <- purrr::imap(weight_options, \(weight, label) {
  stacked |>
    dplyr::mutate(weight_option = weight) |>
    srvyr::as_survey_design(
      ids = psu_id,
      strata = strata_year,
      weights = weight_option,
      check_strata = FALSE
    ) |>
    dplyr::summarise(
      diabetes = srvyr::survey_mean(diabetes_yes, na.rm = TRUE)
    ) |>
    dplyr::mutate(adults_millions = sum(weight) / 1e6)
}) |>
  purrr::list_rbind(names_to = "weights")
rm(weight_options, whole_file_share) # Cache the small table, not the weight vectors.

weight_comparison |>
  dplyr::transmute(
    Weights = weights,
    `Adults (millions)` = adults_millions,
    `Diabetes (%)` = 100 * diabetes,
    `Standard error` = 100 * diabetes_se
  ) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = c(0, 1, 2, 3))

The prevalence hardly changes, because a prevalence is a ratio and the years are of similar size. The population total changes a great deal: unadjusted weights give three times the adult population, so any estimated number of adults with diabetes would be three times too large. Report which rule you used.

11.10 Helpers

Two short functions format the results for the tables below, and one extracts prevalence ratios (PRs) from a model, as in Chapter 9.

Show / hide code 
# Prevalence estimates as percentages; `group` is the column that names each row.
format_prevalence <- function(estimates, group) {
  result <- estimates |>
    dplyr::transmute(
      `Survey year` = {{ group }},
      Respondents = format(n, big.mark = ","),
      `Diabetes (%)` = sprintf("%.2f", 100 * diabetes),
      `Standard error` = sprintf("%.2f", 100 * diabetes_se),
      `95% CI` = sprintf(
        "%.2f to %.2f",
        100 * diabetes_low,
        100 * diabetes_upp
      )
    )
  return(result)
}

# Prevalence ratios with 95% confidence intervals for the terms that match `pattern`.
tidy_pr <- function(fit, pattern) {
  result <- broom::tidy(fit, conf.int = TRUE, exponentiate = TRUE) |>
    dplyr::filter(grepl(pattern, term)) |>
    dplyr::select(term, estimate, conf.low, conf.high, p.value)
  return(result)
}

# Prevalence ratios as text; columns that are not estimates are kept.
format_pr <- function(estimates) {
  result <- estimates |>
    dplyr::mutate(
      `PR (95% CI)` = sprintf(
        "%.2f (%.2f to %.2f)",
        estimate,
        conf.low,
        conf.high
      ),
      p = format.pval(p.value, digits = 2, eps = 0.001),
      .keep = "unused"
    )
  return(result)
}

11.11.2 Do not use the pooled weights for a single year

The pooled weights multiply each state’s weights by a different share in each year. New York’s 2024 respondents keep more than half of their weight and most other states’ about a third, so within 2024 New York would count for more than its population. The year’s estimate moves (Table 11.4).

11.12.1 What pooling buys: a small group

For all adults, one year is already precise. Pooling pays off for small groups. Take adults aged 18 to 34 in Michigan (Table 11.5). The group is selected from the design, not from the data, so that every stratum and PSU stays in the variance calculation (Section 7.5).

The pooled estimate rests on about three times as many respondents, and its standard error is clearly smaller than any single year’s. The price is the interpretation: it is the group’s average prevalence over three years. If the prevalence changed during the period, the average hides the change, so look at the yearly estimates before pooling.

11.13 Did the prevalence change?

A comparison between years should compare the same places. The 2023 file has no respondents from Kentucky or Pennsylvania and the 2024 file none from Tennessee, so a difference between years in the full file mixes a change over time with a change in which states are counted. Here the comparison is limited to the states that are in all three years (Table 11.6). They are selected from the design.

A survey-weighted model with the survey year as a categorical predictor compares each year with 2022. As in Chapter 9, a Poisson model with a log link gives prevalence ratios (Table 11.7). The second model adds age group, because the age distribution of the population also shifts from year to year and diabetes rises steeply with age.

Show / hide code 
crude_fit <- survey::svyglm(
  diabetes_yes ~ survey_year,
  design = design_same_states,
  family = stats::quasipoisson()
)
adjusted_fit <- survey::svyglm(
  diabetes_yes ~ survey_year + age_group,
  design = design_same_states,
  family = stats::quasipoisson()
)
change <- dplyr::bind_rows(
  crude = tidy_pr(crude_fit, "^survey_year"),
  age_adjusted = tidy_pr(adjusted_fit, "^survey_year"),
  .id = "model"
)
# Joint test of the two year coefficients: is any year different from 2022?
year_test <- survey::regTermTest(adjusted_fit, ~survey_year, method = "Wald")
rm(crude_fit, adjusted_fit) # Cache the small results, not the full model objects.

change |>
  format_pr() |>
  knitr::kable(align = "llrr")

The joint test asks whether the prevalence was the same in all three years, at the same age (p 0.0026). In these states, the age-adjusted prevalence in 2024 was 1.03 times the prevalence in 2022 (95% confidence interval 1.01 to 1.06). With samples this large, a small difference can have a small p-value, so judge the size of the ratio, not the p-value alone. Three years are also too few to call a difference a trend.

11.14 A pooled model for an association

Pooled years are often used to estimate an association more precisely. The model below asks how the prevalence of diabetes differs between men and women of the same age, on average over 2022-2024. It uses design_pooled and includes the survey year, so that differences between years in the level of diabetes are not mixed into the comparison.

Before reporting one pooled association, check that it was similar in every year. The second model adds a sex-by-year interaction, and the joint test asks whether the PR for sex differed between years.

Show / hide code 
pooled_fit <- survey::svyglm(
  diabetes_yes ~ sex + age_group + survey_year,
  design = design_pooled,
  family = stats::quasipoisson()
)
pooled_sex <- tidy_pr(pooled_fit, "^sex")

interaction_fit <- survey::svyglm(
  diabetes_yes ~ sex * survey_year + age_group,
  design = design_pooled,
  family = stats::quasipoisson()
)
interaction_test <- survey::regTermTest(
  interaction_fit,
  ~ sex:survey_year,
  method = "Wald"
)

# The same model in 2024 alone, for comparison.
single_fit <- survey::svyglm(
  diabetes_yes ~ sex + age_group,
  design = dplyr::filter(design_years, year == 2024),
  family = stats::quasipoisson()
)
single_sex <- tidy_pr(single_fit, "^sex")
rm(pooled_fit, interaction_fit, single_fit)

dplyr::bind_rows(
  `2024 alone` = single_sex,
  `2022-2024 pooled` = pooled_sex,
  .id = "years"
) |>
  format_pr() |>
  knitr::kable(align = "llrr")

The interaction test gives no evidence that the association differed between years (p 0.96), so one pooled PR is a fair summary (Table 11.8): men had 1.15 times the prevalence of women of the same age (95% confidence interval 1.13 to 1.18). The pooled interval is narrower than the interval from 2024 alone. If the interaction test had shown a difference, report the association for each year instead.

TipOptional modules in several years

Modules add a step. The states that ask a module, and the questionnaire versions they ask it on, can change every year. For each year, build the module file with that year’s version weights and that year’s table of modules by state, as in Section 5.2. Then stack the years and apply the adjustment in Section 11.8 to each year’s adjusted module weight. Shares within each state handle states that asked the module in only some years.

TipMissing data in several years

This chapter’s items have few missing answers. If your analysis needs multiple imputation (Chapter 8), impute the stacked file and include the survey year in the imputation model, because the analysis model includes it.

WarningHow far back can you go?

CDC changed the BRFSS sample (adding cell phones) and its weighting method in 2011, and advises against comparing estimates from before 2011 with later ones. Within the period you choose, each year’s Comparability of Data report lists what changed. CDC also warns that estimates for small groups can be unreliable, and suppresses its own when they rest on fewer than 50 respondents or have a relative standard error above 30%.

11.16 Practice

  1. You stack 2022-2024 and use _LLCPWT unchanged. Which is affected more: the prevalence of diabetes, or the estimated number of adults with diabetes? Why?
  2. Why does this chapter compute the year shares within each state?
  3. A colleague builds the pooled design with strata = STSTR. What does the survey package now assume, and how do you fix it?
  4. You want to know whether diabetes prevalence in Pennsylvania changed between 2022 and 2024. Which weights and which respondents do you use?
  5. A reader concludes from the last section that people’s risk of diabetes rose between 2022 and 2024. What is wrong with that sentence?
TipSuggested answers
  1. The number. A prevalence is a ratio, and multiplying every weight by a similar constant changes its numerator and denominator alike. A total is a sum of weights, so it would be about three times too large.
  2. Sample sizes change from year to year in some states and not in others, and three states are in only two of the years. Shares within a state add up to 1 for every state, so each state counts once whatever years it has.
  3. That respondents from different years with the same stratum code were drawn in one stratum. Number each year-stratum pair, and each year-stratum-PSU triple, and use those as the strata and PSU identifiers.
  4. The original weights (design_years), with Pennsylvania selected from the design (dplyr::filter(state_name == "Pennsylvania")) and the 2022 and 2024 survey years compared. Pennsylvania is not in the 2023 file.
  5. Different people were interviewed each year, so the data cannot show a change in any person’s risk. They show that the prevalence of reported diagnosed diabetes in the adult population was higher in 2024 than in 2022. Changes in diagnosis, survival, and who lives in these states can all contribute to that.
